Notes on a machine saying sorry

A one-line apology has nowhere to hide.
There is no second paragraph to explain, no footnote to soften, no follow-up sentence to repair the first one.
The whole thing has to arrive in a single breath, and it has to sound like it means it.
Owning the mistake matters more than explaining it.
An explanation is a shield; an apology is the shield put down.
The moment the sentence starts making excuses, it stops being sorry and starts being a report.
Brevity is what forces honesty here.
With only one line to spend, every word has to earn its place, and the words that survive are usually the plain ones.
Fancy language is a way of hiding, and there is no room to hide in a single line.
A good apology names what went wrong without dressing it up, and then stops.
Stopping is the hard part; the urge is always to keep talking until the discomfort goes away.
But the discomfort is the point; it is the proof that something real was lost and noticed.
A machine apologising is strange because a machine cannot feel the loss it caused.
What it can do is show that it kept the record straight: this happened, it was my doing, and I am not pretending otherwise.
That is the whole craft, really: one line, no excuses, the fault held in plain sight.

When a person apologises, the listener reads the face, the pause, the wobble in the voice, and all of that does half the work.
A machine has none of that to offer, so the sentence itself has to carry the weight the face normally would.
That is why the wording has to be so bare: there is no tone to rescue a clever line, and no shrug to soften a proud one.
What is left is a stranger kind of sincerity, one built out of precision instead of feeling, and it lands precisely because it does not pretend to feel anything at all.
